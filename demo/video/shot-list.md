# Shot list — what is on screen, in order

Pairs with `narration.md`. Ten segments of voice, **nine clips**, and a still at the end. Record
the screen **silently**; the voice is added afterwards, so a page that loads slowly costs a trim
rather than a retake.

**The whole film is 3:46** — 3:41 of narration plus a five-second closing card — against a
four-minute limit, and only the first four minutes are evaluated. Fourteen seconds of margin.

**Record segments 3 to 8 in one continuous pass**, then cut that pass into clips afterwards: the
browser state carries forward, the history grows for real, and the framing cannot drift if you never
stop. The one place that must stay uncut in the finished film is **clip 4** — pressing Analyze, the
sixteen seconds the model takes, and the critique landing. Cutting inside that beat looks exactly
like hiding the latency, and the criteria reward unedited live execution.

**There is a Spanish version of this document**, written to be worked from with the screen in front
of you, outside the repository at `nexus/dev/GUION_RODAJE_ATELIER.md`. It carries the OBS canvas
settings and the lessons a sibling project paid for. This file stays English because the repository
does.

**The waits below are measured, not guessed.** They come from timing the deployed app through
Playwright on 20 August, and they are the reason the narration for each segment is written long
enough to cover its own dead air:

| Action | Measured wait | What is on screen while you wait |
|---|---|---|
| Deep link or sample click → measurement panel | **3–5 s** | The step indicator moves to *Context*; the overlay renders |
| Press *Analyze Drawing with My Context* → critique | **16–17 s** | The button is spent; nothing else moves. This is what segment 5 exists to cover |
| Upload a non-exercise → refusal | **4–5 s** | Step one, unchanged, then the amber panel appears above the sample grid |
| Cold start, either service | **5–10 s** | A blank page. Never let a judge see this — warm both first |

## The nine clips, and how long each has to be

**The column that governs is "screen must cover", not the last one.** A clip that covers its voice
plus the 2.5 s of air after it is a good clip, however short the take was. The right-hand column is
insurance against a slow response or a clumsy start, not a length the edit needs.

**Nine clips to record, plus one still that already exists.** The last row is the closing card, which
is generated by `make_closing_card.py` and is not a take — so the table has ten rows and you press
record nine times.

Clip 4 is the only one that waits on a model, so it is the only one that can come out short on its
own account. That is what the 70 seconds are for. `assemble.py --check` prints this same table
against what you actually recorded.

**Clips 1 and 2 are one spoken beat, split because the source changes.** Camera and screen are
different OBS scenes, and switching scenes inside a take is how a take gets thrown away. Splitting
the narration instead means each half has its own measured window and its own caption break.

### What is *not* in clip 2, and why

**Not the architecture diagram.** The instinct is to open on it so the viewer knows what they are
looking at, and it is the wrong instinct three times over:

- A diagram before the demonstration is a set of rectangles nobody yet has a reason to care about,
  and it would cost sixteen of the most valuable seconds in the film.
- The video's Google Cloud requirement is *"must demonstrate the backend is running on Google
  Cloud"*. Clip 8 does that with the console and the actual request log — live traffic is stronger
  evidence than a drawing of where traffic would go.
- The diagram is already a **mandatory attachment** on the submission form, where a judge reads it
  at their own pace rather than at 1080p for fifteen seconds.

It appears instead as the **closing card**, where the same rectangles summarise something the viewer
has just watched happen. That is when a diagram earns its keep.

## Before you press record

**Warm both services.** They scale to zero, and a cold container in the middle of a continuous take
costs you the take. Read the state first rather than warming what is already warm:

```powershell
gcloud run services list --project atelier-hack --region europe-west1 `
  --format="table(metadata.name,status.latestReadyRevisionName)"

Invoke-RestMethod https://atelier-agent-3hacbhowpq-ew.a.run.app/api/health
Invoke-RestMethod https://atelier-web-3hacbhowpq-ew.a.run.app/api/health
```

`curl` is not a PowerShell alias in 7.x, so `Invoke-RestMethod` is the honest spelling, and it
parses the JSON — which is what you actually want to read. The agent must report
`"gemini_model":"gemini-3.5-flash"` and `"memory_backend":"firestore"`.

**Then run one full analysis and reload.** The first Vertex call of the day is the slow one. Warming
the path means the 16 seconds in the table above stays 16 and does not become 30.

**Set the interface to English.** The narration is in English, so the screen has to be too — except
for one deliberate shot, below. The picker is top-right and writes a cookie.

**Leave the rail expanded.** It remembers its state, so whatever you leave it as is what the take
opens with. Expanded reads better: the three destinations name themselves.

**Have `not-an-exercise.png` ready.** Any page of handwriting or typed text works. The one used for
the README screenshot is generated by a throwaway script; a photo of a real shopping list is better
on camera, and the gate does not care which.

**Do not clear Firestore.** `level-basic` carries sixteen real exercises and a Cloud Scheduler
digest. The history panel and the progression page are worth more full than empty, and everything in
them was produced by analysing an actual drawing.

## Clip 1 · The gap, on paper — 0:00 – 0:17 · **webcam**

**Visual**: you, holding a technical drawing on paper. An isometric cube or a two-view plate, not a
sketch — the subject is a construction and it should look like one.

The voice names the stack, then says *"an isometric axis is at thirty degrees or it is not."* If you
have a set square, that is the moment to have it on the paper. It is the only frame in the film that
shows what the whole project is actually about, and it costs nothing to stage.

This is the only clip where you are on camera. Nineteen seconds.

> The stack is named here **and again in clip 8**. If you trim this clip for time, clip 8 still
> carries it — but do not cut both. The organisers' checklist asks the video for *"clear
> identification of which Gemini model and agent framework used"*.

### The camera, and the one setting that decides this shot

**Lock the exposure, with the paper already in frame.** A webcam left on automatic meters the scene,
sees a sheet of white paper arrive, closes down, and opens again when the paper moves. On camera that
is a pulse of brightness running through the whole nineteen seconds, and it is a more likely way to
lose this clip than any resolution setting. Frame the shot holding the plate, *then* lock exposure and
white balance, then start recording.

**Record it in OBS, not in the camera's own software.** The other eight clips come out of OBS; a clip
from a second encoder can differ in frame rate, colour range or codec, and that shows as a jump at
the cut. Add a scene with the camera as a **Video Capture Device** and leave the canvas and output
settings exactly as they are for the screen clips — one encoder for all nine.

Logitech's **Logi Capture** is useful as the tuning layer rather than the recording layer: it exposes
exposure lock, white-balance lock and field of view with more control than OBS's device-properties
dialog. Point OBS at its virtual camera and you get both. Two cautions — it is **discontinued**, so
it may not install for a recent camera, and if you do use the virtual camera, close it completely
before recording the screen clips. Two applications contending for one device is a hang waiting to
happen.

The rest is light rather than settings: a 1080p webcam gets noisy and drops its effective frame rate
in a dim room, no configuration compensates for that, and a window behind you turns you into a
silhouette. Hold the plate closer than feels natural — at 1080p it needs to fill a good part of the
frame to read as a construction rather than a piece of paper.

## Clip 2 · The finding — 0:19 – 0:36 · **screen**

**Visual**: `docs/PEDAGOGY.md`, on GitHub. Two places in it, in this order:

1. **§3, the criteria table** — fourteen rows of *public criterion · source · what Atelier does with
   it*: UGR Edificación, USAL Zamora, UPC ETSAB, UPM ETSAM, Universidad de Alicante, Carnegie Mellon
   48-175, Sheridan College. Scroll it slowly. The voice is saying *"I read eighteen published
   syllabi and rubrics"* over this, and the table is what makes that a fact rather than an assertion.
2. **§7 · "And yet nobody quantifies the error"** — hold on the sentence in bold: *"Not one of the
   eighteen sources states a numerical tolerance."* The voice reaches *"not one states a tolerance"*
   here. Let the frame and the sentence land together.

Then, on the last line — *"Atelier automates that check"* — cut to the studio on step one. That is
also where clip 3 begins, so the two can be joined without a visible seam.

**Why a document and not a diagram.** This is the most surprising claim in the video, and the only
evidence for it is documentary. Showing the sources while making the claim is proof; showing an
architecture diagram while making it would be a change of subject. See *What is not in clip 2* above.

Start recording on the studio at step one, English, rail expanded. **Do not stop until clip 7
ends.** Everything below happens in one pass, and you cut it into five clips afterwards.

- The studio is on **Drawing**, step one of three. The indicator is in the top bar.
- Let the eleven samples sit on screen for a beat. The eyebrow above each thumbnail says

### Clip 4 · The measurement — 0:54 – 1:25  ⏺ **do not cut from here to the end of the critique**

- The axis table is already on screen beside the two questions. **Point at the X row**:
  should be 30.0°, measured 36.0°, systematic +6.0°, 17 edges. Y and Z at 0.0°.
- Below it: average 3.1°, max 6.2°, parallelism spread 0.4°, **0 of 33 edges off any axis**.
- Type a short answer into the first textarea. Anything true — *"an isometric cube"* is enough.
- Press **Analyze Drawing with My Context**.

### Clip 4, continued · The critique and its guard — 1:27 – 1:48

- **This is the 16–17 second wait, and it is the reason clips 4 and 5 are one clip.** The narration
  is written to fill exactly it, which is why this segment says what is being *enforced* rather than
  describing something on screen. Cutting here would look like hiding the latency.
- When the critique lands, the badge is top-right of the headline card:
  **Anti-Hallucination: Validated ✓**, with `gemini-3.5-flash` underneath.
- Show Plane A and Plane B side by side. Do not read a whole finding aloud — the voice is already
  talking, and the captions carry the words.

### Clip 5 · The refusal — 1:50 – 2:11

- Click **Analyse another drawing** at the bottom of the critique.
- Upload the page of handwriting. **Wait 4–5 s.**
- The amber panel appears **on step one**, above the sample grid: *"This does not look like a
  descriptive-geometry exercise"*, then the model's own reason, then *"Nothing was measured."*
- Let the reason stay on screen long enough to read. It is Gemini's sentence, not ours, and that is
  the point of the shot.

### Clip 6 · Three systems, three references — 2:13 – 2:49

- Click **`03_1point_error_9deg.png`**. Conic overlay, one vanishing point, per-line colours.
  Average 4.5°, worst line 20.3°.
- Then **`10_diedrico_error_18px.png`**. Ground line, the correspondence table, and the amber
  callout: *the plan sits 18 px sideways from the elevation, 2.2% of the page*.
- You do **not** have to press Analyze for these two. The measurement panel is what the narration is
  describing, and stopping for two more critiques costs another thirty-four seconds.
- The strongest sentence in the script lands here — that the conic engine is the weakest of the
  three, and that one drifted plate scores better than the perfect one. Nothing on screen proves it,
  so let the captions do the work and keep moving.

### Clip 7 · History and level — 2:51 – 2:59

- Back on step one, the panel on the right is the history: sixteen exercises, each with its system
  chip, its one figure, and the critique headline written at the time.
- Switch **Level: Basic → Advanced** and let it settle. Eight seconds, two facts, then stop
  recording.

**End the continuous capture here.** Then cut it into clips 3 to 7.

## Editing

**The screen fits the voice, never the reverse.**

1. Copy the nine clips into `demo/video/takes/`, named as `assemble.py` says.
2. `python demo/video/assemble.py --check` — reports, clip by clip, how many seconds it must cover
   and how many it has. Run this *before* opening an editor.
3. Assemble in **Clipchamp**: the ten MP3s laid out in order with **2.5 s** between them, then each
   clip fitted to its voice. That gap is the number the subtitles were computed against, and it is
   defined once, in `demo/video/_narration.py`.
4. Export 1920×1080.
5. `python demo/video/align.py <export>` — cross-correlates each MP3 against the film's own audio
   and reports where the subtitles expected each voice, where it actually is, and the drift. Add
   `--write-srt` to retime `subtitles.srt` against the film rather than against the plan. A segment
   it cannot find confidently is reported and **nothing is written**.
6. Import `subtitles.srt` last.

`assemble.py` without `--check` builds the film itself from the clips and the audio, cropping
16:10 → 16:9 if a take needs it. That is the alternative to steps 3 and 4, not the plan.

**Takes and exports are gitignored.** They are raw footage, they are large, and neither is source.

## Do not

- **Do not cut inside clip 4.** The criteria reward unedited live execution and that is the
  only part of the video which provides it.
- **Do not narrate a number before it renders.** The audio is a separate track; align it *after* the
  panel appears, not before.
- **Do not force the description-versus-drawing mismatch.** Typing *"the corner of a building"* over
  a one-point sketch sometimes makes the router disagree with the measurement, and Atelier then says
  so and changes nothing. Lovely when it fires. Re-shooting until it does is the thing this project
  is against.
- **Do not promise an improvement curve.** The progression curve is jagged because sixteen real
  drawings are jagged. Read it off the page as it is.
- **Do not re-record to remove the 16-second wait.** It is what the system actually costs. Segment 4
  is written to spend it usefully.
